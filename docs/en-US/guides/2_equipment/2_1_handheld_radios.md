# Handheld Radio Buying Guide

Handheld transceivers (HTs) are the gateway into amateur radio for most new operators. They are portable, self-contained, and let you get on the air immediately after earning your license. This guide covers the major brands, popular models, and what to look for when choosing your first — or next — HT.

## What to Look For

Before diving into specific models, understand the key specifications and features that differentiate handheld radios:

### Frequency Coverage

- **Single-band:** Covers either VHF (144–148 MHz) or UHF (420–450 MHz). Less common today.
- **Dual-band:** Covers both VHF and UHF simultaneously. This is the standard and what most hams want.
- **Tri-band / wideband receive:** Some radios add 220 MHz (1.25 m band) or offer extended receive coverage for airband, marine, FRS, GMRS, and more.

### Power Output

Most HTs offer selectable power levels, typically:

- **Low:** 0.5–1 W (useful for nearby repeaters and conserving battery)
- **Medium:** 2–3 W
- **High:** 5–8 W (maximum for most HTs; a few claim 10 W but this drains batteries rapidly)

More power is not always better. A good antenna and a clear path to the repeater matter far more than an extra watt or two.

### Receiver Quality

This is where cheap radios and expensive radios diverge most. A quality receiver has:

- **Good sensitivity** — the ability to hear weak signals
- **Good selectivity** — the ability to reject strong nearby signals without overloading or producing spurious responses (intermod)
- **Low spurious emissions** — clean transmission that does not interfere with other users

Budget radios often have poor front-end filtering, which means they can be overwhelmed in RF-dense environments like cities or near broadcast towers.

### Build Quality and Water Resistance

Higher-end HTs often carry IPX7 or equivalent water-resistance ratings and use more durable materials. If you plan to use your radio outdoors in harsh conditions, this matters.

### Digital Modes

Some HTs support digital voice protocols:

- **DMR (Digital Mobile Radio):** Open standard, widely used in amateur and commercial radio
- **C4FM / System Fusion:** Yaesu's digital voice system
- **D-STAR:** ICOM's digital voice and data system
- **APRS:** Automatic Packet Reporting System for position reporting and messaging

## Budget Radios

### Baofeng UV-5R

The Baofeng UV-5R is the most widely sold amateur radio in history. At roughly $20–30, it provides a dual-band VHF/UHF transceiver that gets new hams on the air with minimal investment.

**Pros:**

- Extremely inexpensive
- Dual-band VHF/UHF
- Huge aftermarket ecosystem (antennas, batteries, cases, programming cables)
- 4–5 W output power
- Widely documented; countless tutorials available

**Cons:**

- Poor front-end filtering; prone to intermodulation in RF-dense areas
- Spurious emissions may be marginal against regulatory limits
- Mediocre audio quality
- Clunky menu-driven interface (programming via CHIRP software recommended)
- Build quality is basic

**Verdict:** A fine radio to find out if you enjoy the hobby, but plan to upgrade once you are committed.

### Quansheng UV-K5

The Quansheng UV-K5 has rapidly become a favorite among budget-conscious hams, especially after the open-source firmware community embraced it.

**Pros:**

- Inexpensive (~$25–35)
- Open-source firmware (egzumer, fagci) unlocks spectrum analyzer, AM reception, SSB reception, and much more
- Wideband receive coverage (18 MHz – 1.3 GHz with firmware mods)
- Better receiver performance than the Baofeng UV-5R in most tests
- USB-C charging on some revisions
- Active community development

**Cons:**

- Stock firmware is limited; the radio really shines only with aftermarket firmware
- Still a budget radio — receiver will not compete with mid-range or premium HTs
- Build quality is adequate but not rugged
- Aftermarket firmware is not officially supported and could void any warranty

**Verdict:** The best value in budget HTs today. The open-source firmware transforms it into a remarkably capable and educational tool.

### Baofeng UV-K5(8) / UV-5R Plus / GT-5R

Baofeng releases many variants with slightly different model names but similar internals. Generally, these are incremental improvements on the UV-5R with better build quality or added features. Research the specific model and check reviews before purchasing, as quality can vary between production runs.

## Mid-Range Radios

### Yaesu FT-65R / FT-25R

Yaesu's entry-level handhelds offer a significant step up in receiver quality and build from the budget Chinese radios.

**Pros:**

- Solid receiver with better front-end filtering
- Clear, loud audio
- Simple, intuitive operation
- Compact form factor
- MIL-STD 810 rated (FT-65R)
- ~$80–100

**Cons:**

- No wideband receive
- Basic feature set (no APRS, no digital voice)
- Proprietary battery/antenna connectors

**Verdict:** A reliable workhorse if you want a straightforward analog FM handheld that just works.

### Yaesu FT-60R

A long-standing favorite, the FT-60R has been in production for over a decade. It offers wideband receive (from 108 MHz to 999 MHz), excellent audio, a real metal chassis, and an intuitive control layout. While it lacks digital modes, many hams consider it the best pure-analog HT ever made. Expect to pay around $150–170.

### BTECH DMR-6X2 / AnyTone AT-D878UVII Plus

For hams interested in DMR digital voice, these radios offer capable DMR Tier I and II operation alongside analog FM, with features like roaming, APRS (AnyTone), Bluetooth, and large contact databases.

**Pros:**

- Full DMR capability with dual time slot
- Analog FM + DMR in one radio
- GPS and APRS (AnyTone)
- Bluetooth audio (AnyTone)
- Good community support and active development
- ~$150–250

**Cons:**

- DMR programming can be complex for beginners (code plugs)
- Bulkier than simple analog HTs
- The DMR ecosystem requires some study to understand (talk groups, time slots, color codes)

**Verdict:** Excellent choice if you want to explore DMR networks like Brandmeister and TGIF alongside conventional analog repeaters.

## Premium Radios

### Yaesu FT-5DR / FT-3DR

Yaesu's flagship handhelds include C4FM digital voice (System Fusion), wideband receive, touchscreen (FT-3DR), GPS, APRS, Bluetooth, and excellent receiver performance.

**Pros:**

- C4FM digital voice with automatic mode switching (AMS)
- Excellent receiver with narrow front-end filters
- GPS and full APRS functionality
- Wideband receive
- High build quality, IPX5 rated (FT-5DR)
- ~$300–500

**Cons:**

- Expensive
- C4FM is less widely adopted than DMR in some regions
- Proprietary battery and antenna connectors

### Kenwood TH-D75A

The TH-D75A is Kenwood's latest flagship HT and supports D-STAR, FM analog, and wideband receive. It includes a built-in TNC for APRS, GPS, Bluetooth, and a color display.

**Pros:**

- D-STAR digital voice with terminal and access point modes
- Excellent receiver, wide frequency coverage
- Built-in TNC for APRS and packet
- Bluetooth, GPS, micro-SD card slot
- IF output for panadapter use
- Premium build quality
- ~$500–600

**Cons:**

- The most expensive HT on the market
- D-STAR infrastructure is less common than DMR in many areas

### ICOM ID-52A

ICOM's flagship portable supports D-STAR, FM, and offers features like GPS, Bluetooth audio, a color display, and built-in audio recording. The D-STAR terminal mode allows internet-linked communication through your smartphone.

**Pros:**

- D-STAR with terminal mode and DR function for easy repeater linking
- IPX7 waterproof
- Excellent build and audio quality
- microSD recording
- ~$450–550

**Cons:**

- Pricey
- D-STAR availability varies by region

## Recommended Accessories for HTs

Regardless of which HT you choose, consider these upgrades:

1. **Aftermarket antenna:** The rubber duck antenna included with most HTs is a compromise. A longer whip antenna like the Nagoya NA-771 or Signal Stick dramatically improves performance.
2. **Programming cable and software:** Use CHIRP (free, open-source) to program memories and settings via your computer instead of struggling with the radio's keypad.
3. **Extended battery or battery eliminator:** For extended use, a higher-capacity battery or a 12 V eliminator cable is valuable.
4. **Speaker-microphone:** A remote speaker-mic keeps the radio on your belt or pack while giving you easy access to audio and PTT.
5. **Carry case or chest harness:** Protects the radio and keeps it accessible.

## Summary Recommendations

| Budget | Recommended Model | Key Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Under $40 | Quansheng UV-K5 | Best value, open-source firmware |
| $80–$170 | Yaesu FT-65R or FT-60R | Reliable, great receiver, simple |
| $150–$250 | AnyTone AT-D878UVII Plus | DMR + analog, APRS, Bluetooth |
| $300–$600 | Yaesu FT-5DR or Kenwood TH-D75A | Premium features, digital voice, APRS |

Choose the radio that fits your budget and goals. Remember that any radio gets you on the air, and the best radio is the one you actually use.
