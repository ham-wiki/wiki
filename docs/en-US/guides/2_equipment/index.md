# Equipment Guide

Welcome to the amateur radio equipment guide. Whether you are just getting started with your first handheld or building out a full home station, choosing the right equipment is one of the most important decisions you will make in the hobby. This section walks you through every major category of gear, explains what to look for, and offers concrete recommendations at various price points.

## Equipment Categories

### Handheld Radios (HTs)

Handheld transceivers — commonly called HTs — are the most accessible entry point into amateur radio. They are compact, battery-powered, and typically cover the VHF (144 MHz) and UHF (430 MHz) bands. An HT lets you hit local repeaters, participate in nets, and provide communications during community events or emergencies. Prices range from under $30 for budget Chinese radios to over $500 for flagship Japanese models.

[Read the Handheld Radio Buying Guide](./2_1_handheld_radios.md)

### Mobile Radios

Mobile transceivers are designed for vehicle installation but also make excellent base station radios when paired with a power supply. They offer significantly more transmit power than handhelds (typically 25–50 W), better receivers, and richer feature sets including dual-band monitoring, GPS/APRS integration, and digital voice modes like C4FM and D-STAR.

[Read the Mobile Radio Guide](./2_2_mobile_radios.md)

### HF Transceivers

HF (high-frequency) radios operate on the 1.8–30 MHz bands and open the door to worldwide communication. Bouncing signals off the ionosphere, HF operators routinely make contacts across continents using modest power and simple wire antennas. Modern HF transceivers include built-in SDR waterfalls, DSP noise reduction, and automatic antenna tuners.

[Read the HF Transceivers Guide](./2_3_hf_radios.md)

### Software Defined Radio (SDR)

SDR receivers and transceivers replace traditional analog circuitry with digital signal processing in software. For as little as $25, an RTL-SDR dongle turns your computer into a wideband receiver covering 24 MHz to 1.7 GHz. More advanced SDR platforms like the HackRF and SDRPlay open up transmit capabilities and wider bandwidth.

[Read the SDR Guide](./2_4_sdr.md)

### Accessories

No station is complete without the supporting accessories: coaxial cables, connectors, SWR meters, power supplies, dummy loads, and more. Choosing quality accessories — and understanding why they matter — can make the difference between a frustrating experience and a reliable, high-performance station.

[Read the Accessories Guide](./2_5_accessories.md)

## Choosing Your First Radio

If you are newly licensed and wondering where to start, here is a simple decision tree:

| Goal | Recommended Starting Point |
|---|---|
| Talk on local repeaters, get on the air quickly | Budget handheld (Quansheng UV-K5, Baofeng UV-5R) |
| Reliable local/regional comms, mobile or base use | Dual-band mobile radio (Yaesu FTM-300DR, ICOM IC-2730) |
| Work the world on HF | Entry-level HF transceiver (ICOM IC-7300, Yaesu FT-991A) |
| Listen and learn before transmitting | RTL-SDR dongle + free software |

## Budget Considerations

Amateur radio equipment ranges from extremely affordable to very expensive. A few principles to keep in mind:

- **The antenna matters more than the radio.** A modest radio with a good antenna will outperform an expensive radio with a poor antenna every time.
- **Buy used.** The amateur radio used market is active and radios hold their value well. Check hamfests, online swap meets, and forums.
- **Start simple, upgrade later.** You do not need the most feature-rich radio on day one. Start with something you can learn on and upgrade when you know what you actually need.
- **Factor in the full station cost.** A radio alone is not a station. Budget for an antenna, feedline, connectors, a power supply (for mobile/HF rigs), and possibly a tuner.
